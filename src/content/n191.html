Running load tests that produce trustworthy, actionable results.

The tool basics are in chapter 110. Here's what makes a test realistic:

  • A realistic traffic mix: derive the ratios from production logs (70% browse, 20% cart, 8% checkout, 2% admin), not one endpoint hammered in a loop.
  • Realistic data: production-sized tables, varied IDs (so caches aren't artificially hot), realistic payload sizes.
  • Open vs closed models: real users arrive at a rate regardless of how slow you are (an open model: k6 constant-arrival-rate). A fixed number of virtual users waiting for responses (a closed model) hides overload by slowing its own request rate.
  • Warm-up before measuring (JIT compilers, caches, connection pools).
  • Watch the server side during the test: CPU, memory, DB connections, queue depth, GC pauses. The client tells you that it's slow; the server tells you why.
  • Find the knee: increase load step by step until p99 latency or errors bend upwards. That's your real capacity. Plan to run at 50–70% of it.
  • Little's law for sizing: in-flight requests = arrival rate × latency. That tells you the concurrency, pool and thread counts you need.

Practice

191.1 think Your load test used 100 virtual users with no think time and showed 900 requests/s. Why might production still fall over at 400 requests/s?

A closed-loop test slows itself down, hiding overload. The real traffic mix differs (heavier endpoints). Caches were warm. Production data volume is bigger. Test with an open arrival rate and a realistic mix.
